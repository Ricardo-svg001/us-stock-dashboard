## The two tools answer different questions

The Pro section currently has two prototype features. Many people assume they do the same thing — "find strong stocks" — but they don't.

- [New-high screening](/pro?lang=en) asks: **where does this stock stand relative to its own past?**
- [RS relative strength ranking](/pro/rs?lang=en) asks: **how fast is this stock moving compared with other US stocks over the same window?**

One is **absolute strength**, the other **relative strength**. Each covers the other's blind spot.

A stock can have very high RS while sitting far below its prior high — meaning it merely fell less than others, or just bounced out of a deep hole with a wall of trapped supply overhead. Conversely, a stock can have just made a 3-month high with an RS of only 60 — meaning everything rallied and it simply came along, without being particularly strong.

**Stocks that satisfy both conditions are far fewer, but that combination is what "the market is actually buying this" looks like.**

## First, what RS is and isn't

This has to come first, because RS is one of the most misread indicator names in the business.

**RS is not RSI.** RSI is an oscillator computed from a single stock's own moves, telling you about overbought and oversold. RS ignores all of that.

**RS is also not IBD's official RS Rating.** That is a proprietary commercial metric with its own algorithm and database. The RS here is our own price percentile — similar in concept, different in the actual numbers.

The definition used on this site is simple: take the **300 largest US stocks by market cap**, compare their **price returns** over a chosen window, and convert to a percentile from 1 to 99. Weakest is 1, strongest is 99.

So **RS 90 means this stock's return over the window beat roughly 90% of the stocks with usable data.**

Three things to keep in mind:

- **Price only — no dividends, no fundamentals.** It measures where money is flowing, not whether the company is good.
- **The universe is fixed at the top 300.** This is relative strength *within large caps*, not a whole-market ranking. Small and mid-cap movers aren't in the pool.
- **RS is a relative score, not an absolute return.** In a bear market, RS 99 can still be negative — it just fell less than everything else. **This is RS's biggest trap**, and it comes up again below.

RS is computed after the close as part of the daily update, so the button responds immediately — no waiting for data.

## Four windows are really four different questions

RS offers 20, 60, 120 and 250 trading days. The default is 60.

- **20 days (short term)**: money flow over the past month. Fast-moving and noisy — useful for spotting something that has just started.
- **60 days (swing)**: about a quarter. **The default, and the most practical one**, matching the holding period of most momentum positions.
- **120 days (mid term)**: about six months. Long enough to separate a real trend from a single bounce.
- **250 days (long term)**: about a year. Shows who led the market across the whole year.

Any single window is incomplete on its own. **The useful move is to read two windows against each other:**

- **Long high + short high**: the leaders are still leading; the trend continues.
- **Long high + short low**: a former leader is fading. These are the most dangerous, because the reputation lingers after the money has left.
- **Long low + short high**: could be a laggard catching up, or a new group just starting. Judge it alongside new highs and sector — if several names in the same sector strengthen together, that's worth attention.

The method is easy: on the same day, run 250-day and then 20-day, and compare which stocks appear on both lists and which appear only once.

## Using the new-high screen, and one limitation you must know

[New-high screening](/pro?lang=en) also draws from the 300 largest US stocks, over the **last 1, 3 or 5 sessions**. Qualifying on **any single day** within the window counts; results show the actual date and the **highest tier** reached.

**⚠️ This differs from the Taiwan version.**

The US version has six tiers — **3 months / 6 months / 1 year / 2 years / 3 years / 5 years** — with **5 years as the maximum. There is no "all-time high."** The reason is simple: the historical price cache here holds 1,260 trading days (about 5 years), and we shouldn't claim a longer-horizon high than the data supports.

So don't equate a US "5-year high" with the Taiwan site's "all-time high." **It only means "the highest within the 5 years we can see"** — the price may well have been higher 6 years ago. For a longer view, check a monthly chart yourself.

📌 That said, **5 years covers a complete bull-bear cycle** (including the whole of the 2022 bear market). A stock making a 5-year high has cleared the trapped supply from that entire stretch — a different order of significance from a 3-month high.

**A 2% tolerance applies**: a close within 98% of the window's prior high counts as a new high. The exact breakout day is hard to catch, and the process of pressing against the prior high while absorbing supply is often the more useful signal.

Above the results there are two dropdowns — **sector** and **new-high tier** — and these matter more than the list itself:

**Use the sector dropdown to spot groups.** If three or five names from the same sector appear on the new-high list, that's usually not a single-stock move but a whole group turning. Group moves persist far better than isolated ones. Conversely, a stock making highs while none of its peers move deserves extra scepticism.

**Use the tier dropdown to set your bar.** A 3-month high is worth watching; one year or more carries real weight.

## The full sequence

Here's the practical workflow that strings the site's tools together. The point is that **every step narrows the field — no step goes looking for new candidates.**

**Step 1: check which stage the market is in.** Open ["Today's Market"](/?lang=en) on the home page and see where the lifecycle currently sits among the six positions. This step decides not *what* to buy but **whether to buy, and how much**. In a headwind stage, the RS 90 list still has dozens of names — that doesn't make them actionable.

**Step 2: narrow with RS.** Start at 60 days, RS ≥90. Too many names, raise to ≥95; too few, drop to ≥80. **Adjust the threshold before changing the window** — changing the window changes the question, while the threshold only changes how tight the filter is.

**Step 3: confirm position with new highs.** Cross-check the RS list against the new-high list and **prioritise names on both**. This confirms the stock isn't just moving fast but also has no overhead supply.

**Step 4: converge by sector.** Within the intersection, see which sector appears most often. Names clustered in the same group move up the priority list.

**Step 5: confirm trend structure with [the screener](/screener?lang=en).** Check whether the 10/20/50/150-day averages are in bullish alignment. RS and new highs are **outcomes**; the moving average alignment tells you whether those outcomes rest on a stable trend.

**Step 6: wait for an entry with [pullback screening](/pullback?lang=en).** Chasing on the breakout day usually means buying at the worst spot. Waiting for a pullback into ±3% of a moving average produces a far better risk-reward ratio.

**Step 7: decide your exit before you enter.** ⚠️ **The US risk page is still in development** — there is no ATR stop calculator yet (the Taiwan site has one). Until then you have to set stops yourself, but **write them down before buying, not after you're underwater**. You can set a closing-price alert in [notifications](/alerts?lang=en) as a reminder mechanism.

## Adjust by market stage instead of reusing one parameter set daily

The same parameters mean entirely different things in different environments:

- **Tailwind trend** (index > 50MA > 100MA): RS ≥90 over 60 days, paired with new highs, traded normally. This is where the workflow works best.
- **Bull pullback** (50MA > index > 100MA): lead with **new highs + pullback entries**, with RS as support. Short-window RS whipsaws during pullbacks and becomes less informative.
- **High-risk consolidation**: raise the bar to ≥95, keep only the strongest, and cut size. A shorter list is information, not a malfunction.
- **Headwind market** (index < 100MA): **the most valuable judgement here is "don't."** RS is relative — RS 99 in a bear market may well be −10% while everything else is −25%. Treating the RS list as a buy list here means picking the slowest fallers in a downtrend. That's the textbook misuse.
- **Early / confirmed recovery**: this is when **250-day RS earns its keep**. Coming off a bottom, whichever names held high long-window RS through the entire decline are the candidates to lead the next advance.

## Four common misuses

**1. Treating RS as a buy signal.** High RS isn't a reason to buy; it's a reason to look twice. It contains no entry point, no stop level and no time frame — you have to supply all three.

**2. Always taking the very highest RS names.** RS 99 is the most crowded position in the market. Crowded isn't automatically dangerous, but it does mean **most people are already in**, leaving limited marginal buying. A stock at RS 85–95 that just made a 1-year high often sits in a more comfortable spot.

**3. Chasing extended stocks with 20-day RS.** The highest short-window RS names are usually the ones that ran hardest over the past few days. Buying there puts your stop as far away as it can be.

**4. Forgetting the universe is only 300 names.** This RS only answers "who is strongest among large-cap US stocks." Genuine small-cap movers aren't in the pool — not seeing them doesn't mean they don't exist.

## Known limitations, stated upfront

- **Both features are prototypes**, not yet tied to subscription access, and the conditions may change.
- **New highs top out at 5 years** (1,260 trading days of history). There is no "all-time high" tier.
- **The RS universe is *today's* top 300.** Membership shifts with market cap, so today's RS and RS three months from now aren't measured against exactly the same peer group.
- **Both new highs and RS use closing prices only.** The intraday price shown in tables is display-only and never affects screening — otherwise the same conditions would produce different answers at different times of day.
- **This workflow has not been fully backtested.** It organises the logic of using the tools; it is not verified performance. Any workflow that reads smoothly still has to be recorded and reviewed against your own real trades.

## One last thing

New highs and RS both do exactly one job: **shrink several hundred stocks down to a number you can actually read.**

They won't tell you when to buy, how much to buy, or when to admit you were wrong. Those three are permanently your work — and they matter far more to your results than whether you used RS 90 or RS 95.
