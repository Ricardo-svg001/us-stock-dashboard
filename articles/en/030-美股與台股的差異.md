## How the two sites relate

[US Stock Coffee](/?lang=en) is the US edition of [Stock Coffee](https://stock-coffee.com). The core logic is identical: **use moving averages to find stocks that are trending, then wait for a pullback toward the average to enter.**

But moving that logic to US equities means **half the parameters have to change**. Not for the sake of looking different — the two markets genuinely differ in data structure and trading mechanics, and copying the settings across produces wrong conclusions.

Each item below explains why the change was necessary.

## 1. Moving averages: 5/10/20/60 becomes 10/20/50/150

The Taiwan version uses 5, 10, 20 and 60 days; the US version uses **10, 20, 50 and 150**.

This isn't arbitrary. Taiwan investors talk about the "weekly, monthly and quarterly line," where the quarterly line is 60 days and marks the mid-term trend. In US markets the **50-day average** occupies exactly that role — institutions, financial media and technical analysis texts all reference the 50-day.

The **150-day average** has no Taiwan counterpart here. Both O'Neil's and Minervini's trend templates use it to confirm whether a stock remains in a bullish structure. US swings run longer than Taiwan's, so an extra slow line is needed to see the mid-to-long-term trend.

📌 In practice: **wherever you'd look at the 60-day line in Taiwan, look at the 50-day line in the US.** The 150-day is an additional filter layer, not a replacement for anything.

The underlying principle is identical in both markets (a moving average is a group's average cost). The Taiwan site's [What is a moving average](https://stock-coffee.com/article/moving-average-basics) covers the basics, and that part transfers directly.

## 2. Reading the market: Taiwan uses margin balance, the US uses breadth

This is the largest difference between the two sites.

Stock Coffee uses **margin balance** to judge whether retail leverage has cooled — a genuine advantage specific to Taiwan, because margin data is published daily and denominated in share lots, directly reflecting retail leverage.

**The US has no equivalent.** FINRA margin debt is monthly and denominated in dollars — and dollar amounts inflate along with prices, which is exactly the problem the Taiwan version deliberately avoids.

So the US version uses **market breadth** instead: the percentage of the top 300 stocks trading above their own 150-day average. Same concept (measuring internal market health), and computable from price data already on hand.

⚠️ **But the two indicators behave completely differently, so the same rules cannot apply.**

Margin balance is a **slow variable**: after prices bounce, margin stays depressed for months. Breadth is a **fast variable**: it recovers almost in step with price.

Porting the Taiwan "bottom" rule directly produces a state that almost never occurs, because "low breadth" and "back above the average" rarely coincide. In backtesting, the copied rule left the bottom state occupying just 1.4% of the time. The US version therefore looks at the **lowest breadth over the past 90 days** — "was there a washout this quarter?" — which brings the figure back to a reasonable range.

📌 **This is the most valuable lesson here**: changing the data source behind a concept usually means rewriting the rule, not renaming a field.

The US market states are finally arranged into a **six-stage lifecycle** (early recovery → confirmed recovery → tailwind trend → bull pullback → high-risk consolidation → headwind market), which is what "Today's Market" on the home page shows. The division of labour: **50MA for tailwinds, 100MA for whether headwinds have genuinely arrived, 150MA breadth for whether a washout has occurred.** Details are in [Reading the US Market](/en/article/us-market-breadth-150ma).

## 3. New highs: Taiwan sees 6 years, the US sees 5

The Taiwan version has five tiers: 6 months, 1 year, 3 years, 5 years and **all-time high**.

The US version has six: 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, 3 years and **5 years** — **capped at 5 years, with no "all-time high."**

The reason is simple: the US historical price cache holds 1,260 trading days (about 5 years). Without earlier data, we shouldn't claim a longer-horizon high. The Taiwan version can label an all-time high because its data source reaches back 6 years.

⚠️ **So don't read a US "5-year high" as a Taiwan-style "all-time high."** It only means "the highest within the 5 years we can see" — the price may well have been higher 6 years ago. For a longer view, check a monthly chart yourself.

📌 That said, 5 years **covers a complete bull-bear cycle** (including the whole of the 2022 bear market). A stock making a 5-year high has cleared the trapped supply from that entire stretch.

Both versions use the same **2% tolerance**: a close within 98% of the window's prior high counts. The exact breakout day is hard to catch, and the process of pressing against the prior high while absorbing supply is often the more useful signal. The Taiwan site's [New highs and the 2% tolerance](https://stock-coffee.com/article/new-high-tiers-and-tolerance) covers the reasoning in more depth, and it applies identically here.

## 4. Fundamentals: monthly revenue vs quarterly reports

Taiwan-listed companies **must publish revenue every month** — a rare requirement globally. So the Taiwan version can use monthly revenue YoY, refreshed once a month.

**The US has no monthly revenue**, only quarterly reports. So the US version uses **quarterly revenue and EPS year-over-year**, refreshed once a quarter.

📌 The practical difference: Taiwan's fundamental signals are more timely, the US ones more lagged. When screening on US quarterly YoY, remember the number may already describe events two or three months old.

## 5. P/E ratio: deliberately omitted from the US version

The Taiwan version shows P/E; the US version **deliberately removed it**.

It existed once, but the data source's EPS **could not be confirmed as GAAP or adjusted**. The two produce very different P/E ratios, and AAPL once came out with an obviously wrong number.

**Better to have one fewer column than one wrong number.** This is a deliberate trade-off, not an unfinished feature.

## 6. Trading mechanics: price limits and stops

This isn't a difference between the websites but between the markets — and it directly affects how you trade.

**Taiwan has a ±10% daily price limit.** At limit-down, a sell order may not fill at all — so a stop can exist and still fail to execute. That's the single largest risk to stops in Taiwan.

**US single stocks have no daily price limit**, only LULD (limit up-limit down) bands that briefly halt trading in extreme moves. So a US stock **can fall 30% or 40% in a day** on an earnings miss or a failed clinical trial.

📌 The risk shapes are exact opposites: **Taiwan's fear is "I can't get out"; the US fear is "there is no floor."** US trading therefore depends more on position sizing than on the stop price alone.

## 7. Time zones: you simply can't watch the screen

Taiwan trades 09:00–13:30 local time; you can watch it.

The US trades 09:30–16:00 ET, which in Taiwan time is **21:30–04:00 in summer and 22:30–05:00 in winter**. Unless you skip sleep, you will miss the second half.

📌 This determines strategy in reverse: **if you can't watch, don't use methods that require watching.** Every screen on this site uses **closing prices** (the live price is display-only and never affects screening), and price alerts are checked after the close — all to fit the reality that your decisions happen after the close.

If you want to day-trade US equities, this site isn't for you, and the time difference makes it less so.

## 8. Ex-dividend: Taiwan's summer gap

Taiwan's ex-dividend season is concentrated in **July through September**. On the ex-date the price drops by the dividend, leaving a gap on the chart. When the amount is large (5%–8% is common for high-yield names), it distorts both moving-average reads and new-high detection — it *looks* like a break below the average when it's only the dividend.

The US pays **quarterly, in smaller amounts** (mostly 0.5%–1.5%), with far less technical impact — usually negligible in practice.

📌 This is why Taiwan traders must scrutinise summer signals for authenticity while US traders largely don't.

## 9. Costs and taxes: rules change, so only the shape is given here

- **Taiwan**: a securities transaction tax on sales (0.3% for ordinary shares) plus brokerage commission. Costs are explicit and paid every time.
- **US**: most US brokers and some sub-brokerage channels are zero- or low-commission with no transaction tax; but there is a **currency cost** (the TWD/USD spread), and dividends paid to Taiwan investors are typically subject to **30% withholding tax**.

⚠️ Tax and fee rules change, and terms differ across brokers, sub-brokerage arrangements and overseas accounts. **The above is a reminder that these line items exist, not tax or investment advice.** Confirm against your broker and the relevant authorities.

## So does the strategy need to change?

The core logic doesn't: **find stocks that are trending, wait for a pullback toward the average, decide your exit before entering.** That holds in both markets.

What changes are the **parameters and the rhythm**:

- The moving-average positions shift (60 → 50, plus a new 150)
- The basis for reading the market changes (margin → breadth), and the rule must be rewritten
- The new-high horizon shortens — don't overstate what a tier means
- Fundamental data arrives more slowly — don't treat it as a live signal
- Decisions are fixed to after the close, because the time difference gives you no alternative

📌 **The easiest mistake is porting the parameter set that works well in Taiwan and then blaming US markets for being "inaccurate."** They aren't inaccurate — that parameter set is answering a different market's question.

To check both markets on the same day, the toggle in the top-right jumps straight to Stock Coffee. The two sites compute their states independently — **don't infer the US state from the Taiwan state, or vice versa.**
