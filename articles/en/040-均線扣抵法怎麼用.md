## A fact many people never notice

Whether a moving average rises or falls **is already decided today**, regardless of what price does tomorrow.

It sounds odd, but it's only arithmetic. The 50-day average is the mean of the last 50 closes. To compute tomorrow's value, two things happen: **the close from 50 days ago is dropped**, and tomorrow's close is added.

That bar about to be dropped is the **deduction value**.

So:

- Deduction value **below the current price** → drop a small number, add a large one → **the 50-day line must rise**
- Deduction value **above the current price** → drop a large number, add a small one → **the 50-day line must fall**

As a formula:

**Tomorrow's MA = today's MA + (tomorrow's close − deduction value) ÷ 50**

So once you know the deduction value, you can make the call today. No forecast required.

## What it's for: estimating how much consolidation time is left

The most common use of a moving average is as support. After a stock advances and then moves sideways, the 50-day line gradually catches up. **The moment it arrives, support is pressed right against price** — and the market usually has to pick a side: break out on volume and continue, or break the average and end the move.

So "how many days until the 50-day line reaches the current price" is really answering:

> **How much longer can this consolidation last?**

That's what the [moving-average deduction](/deduction?lang=en) page computes. Choose the Nasdaq Composite or a single stock, enter a price to watch (leave it blank to use the latest close), and it returns the number of sessions for the 50, 100 and 150-day lines.

## Why these three lines

These are the three used elsewhere on the site; this page just points them at the same question:

- The **50-day line** occupies the position in US markets that the quarterly line does in Taiwan — institutions, financial media and technical texts all reference it.
- The **100-day line** is what "Today's Market" on the home page uses to decide whether **headwinds have genuinely arrived**.
- The **150-day line** is the trend-template line (O'Neil and Minervini), and the one used by [the screener](/screener?lang=en) and by market breadth.

⚠️ All three are computed because each has a job on this site. When the home page says "below the 100-day line = headwind," you can look up here how many days until the 100-day line reaches this price — the same vocabulary throughout.

## How to read the page

**The top two rows are the conclusion:**

- **Consolidation**: assumes price stays flat. This is the **slowest** case for the average to catch up, so the number of days is an **upper bound**.
- **Continuing the 20-day slope**: assumes price keeps moving at its recent pace.

The reasonable range sits between the two numbers.

⚠️ **Be careful when the slope is negative.** The "trend" row will show fewer days — but **not because the average is catching up faster; because price is falling into the average.** The meaning is exactly reversed. The page prints an extra note when it detects a negative slope; when you see it, read the "consolidation" row instead.

**Below that are the details** explaining where the number came from: the average's current level, tomorrow's deduction bar, and whether that value sits below or above the target price.

Three outcomes have three phrasings: **"already deducted"** means the average has reached the level today; **"more than 150 sessions"** means it can't catch up within the computable range; otherwise you get a day count.

## A concrete example

Say a stock trades at $100, the 50-day line sits at 95, and tomorrow's deduction value is 88.

The deduction value of 88 is below the $100 price, so the 50-day line will rise. How fast? If price simply holds at 100, each day drops an old value around 88 and adds a new one at 100, lifting the average by roughly $0.24 a day. Covering the $5 from 95 to 100 takes about 21 sessions — roughly a month.

**So you have about a month of consolidation room**; until then the 50-day line sits below price and applies no pressure.

Conversely, if the deduction value were 105 (above the current price), the average would decline toward a price at 100 — an average pressing down from above, which means something entirely different.

## ⚠️ This is arithmetic extrapolation, not prediction

This has to be stated plainly, because it's where the tool gets misused.

The deduction value itself is **certain** — that bar is already in history and won't change. But the "catches up in N days" answer rests on the assumption that price moves at the same rate every day, and real markets don't.

So read the day count as **"at this pace, roughly this long"** — not "guaranteed this many days."

## While we're on the subject: breaking below an average

Since we're discussing moving averages, let's clear up a common misconception. We backtested Nasdaq Composite data going back to 1971, and the question splits two ways — **with answers an order of magnitude apart.**

**Question 1: "It broke below today — what happens next?"**

Treating every break as an independent event, the median subsequent decline is only about **−2%**, and **70–90% reclaim the average within 20 sessions.**

In other words, **a single break is overwhelmingly noise.** Selling everything on a break usually means getting shaken out.

**Question 2: "In this correction, how much further from the break to the low?"**

Here we first identify complete cycles — the index tops out, weakens, breaks the long-term average, and runs to that cycle's low — and take only the **last break before the bottom**. On that basis, the median across 6 cycles in the past decade is roughly **−19.8%** further after breaking the 100-day line and **−16.8%** after the 150-day.

The past decade, case by case:

- 2018-08 high → 2018-10-08 break of the 100-day → 2018-12-24 low, a further **−19.9%**
- 2020-02 high → 2020-03-05 break → 2020-03-23 low, a further **−21.5%**
- 2021-11 high → 2022-01-05 break → 2022-03-14 low, a further −16.7%
- 2022-08 high → 2022-09-13 break → 2022-12-28 low, a further −12.2%
- 2024-12 high → 2025-02-25 break → 2025-04-08 low, a further **−19.8%**

⚠️⚠️ **Both numbers are correct, but they answer different questions and must never be mixed.**

Question 2 uses the information "which break was the last one" — and that is **only knowable afterward**. In the moment you cannot tell whether the break in front of you is the final one. So the −20% figure is for **setting expectations** (this is roughly what a real correction looks like), not for deciding whether to sell today.

Liquidating because "it drops another 20% after a break" is exactly the error of treating question 2 as question 1 — and question 1's real answer is "70–90% reclaim it."

📌 Incidentally, **the 100-day line ran 1.3 to 3 percentage points deeper than the 150-day in all three periods of question 2.** The reason is visible: the 100-day breaks earlier (down an average of −7.7% at the break versus −9.3% for the 150-day), and **breaking earlier leaves more to come**. That is also why the home page uses the 100-day line as the headwind line.

## Three practical suggestions

**1. Treat deduction as a timetable, not a signal.** It tells you when the average will arrive, not whether to buy or sell.

**2. Use it alongside a view you already hold.** If you already like a stock and are waiting for an entry, "the 50-day line needs 20 more days" means you have time. If you already want to trim, "it presses in within 3 days" is a reason to move faster.

**3. Look further ahead than tomorrow.** This page computes tomorrow's deduction, but you can extend it yourself: if the next several bars due for deduction are all high prices, the average will flatten or turn down — even if it still looks like it's rising today.
