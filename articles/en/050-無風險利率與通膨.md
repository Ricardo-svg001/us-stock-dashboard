## A very common shortcut

"Bond yields and inflation are roughly the same anyway, so I'll just use the bond yield as my inflation number."

The direction is usually right — the two do move together. But the shortcut skips the piece that decides whether your money actually grew.

Separate the questions first:

- **Inflation** answers: **how much purchasing power am I losing each year?**
- **The risk-free rate** answers: **what can my money earn without taking much risk?**

One is a cost, the other a return. Related, not the same number.

## Why you can't set them equal

**Case one: inflation 3%, risk-free rate 2%.**

Your cash earns 2% while prices rise 3%. On paper you have more; in practice you can buy less. **Positive nominal, negative real.**

**Case two: inflation 2%, risk-free rate 4%.**

Real return:

(1 + 4%) ÷ (1 + 2%) − 1 ≈ 1.96%

Note the numerator is **1 + 4%**, not 4%. Dividing two growth rates requires adding 1 to both sides — an easy formula to get wrong.

## The correlation is structural, not coincidental

A nominal rate breaks roughly into three parts — the **Fisher relation**:

Nominal rate ≈ real rate + expected inflation + inflation risk premium

So **a bond yield already contains the market's expectation of future inflation**. Anyone lending at a fixed rate for ten years demands compensation for rising prices first, or the money returned has shrunk.

The gap between the two is the **real interest rate** — the number worth watching.

That gives a practical test: **when the nominal yield sits below inflation, the market is accepting a negative real rate.** Sitting in cash "waiting for an opportunity" then costs more than it appears, because you aren't earning zero — you're earning less.

## The actual US numbers

From the [Rates and Purchasing Power](/macro?lang=en) page (August 2026):

- US 2-year Treasury: **4.20%**
- US 10-year Treasury: **4.68%**
- US cumulative CPI over ten years: **+38.61%**, roughly **3.32%** annualized

Real return on the 10-year:

(1 + 4.68%) ÷ (1 + 3.32%) − 1 ≈ **+1.32%**

The 2-year is around **+0.85%**.

**Both are positive.** Holding US Treasuries today gains purchasing power slowly — though not by much.

## Compared with Taiwan: same method, opposite conclusion

This is the section most worth remembering.

In [the companion article on Stock Coffee](https://stock-coffee.com/article/risk-free-rate-vs-inflation), Taiwan's 10-year real return comes out at **−0.39%**. Same formula, same logic, opposite conclusion.

But **don't jump to a conclusion — there's a trap here**: that −0.39% uses Taiwan's **five-year** annualized inflation (2.36%), while the US figure above uses **ten-year** (3.32%). Different windows can't be compared.

Aligning both on ten years:

- Taiwan: ten-year annualized inflation about **1.61%**, 10-year bond 1.97% → real about **+0.35%**
- US: ten-year annualized inflation about **3.32%**, 10-year bond 4.68% → real about **+1.32%**

**Once aligned, both are positive — but the US is clearly higher.**

📌 The correction itself is the point: **the same numbers flip from negative to positive when you change the window.** Anyone quoting a "real return" must first state which period the inflation assumption came from.

⚠️ And this **still doesn't make US bonds the better deal.** That 4.68% is denominated in US dollars; a Taiwan investor converts in and back out. A 5% appreciation in the Taiwan dollar wipes out the entire spread and more. **Comparing rates across currencies compares two different things**, unless you account separately for hedging costs.

## Three lines: splitting "did I make money" into three questions

Rather than merging inflation and the risk-free rate into one number, keep them apart and add a third. Ask in order:

**Line one: inflation — the purchasing power floor.**

Did this beat rising prices? If not, the number on your statement grew but what you can buy shrank. A minimum bar, not a passing grade.

**Line two: the risk-free rate — the opportunity cost of capital.**

Was the risk worth taking? If the return matches a government bond, you absorbed volatility and drawdowns without being paid for them.

"Investment return − government bond yield of similar maturity and currency" is a rough **excess return**. ⚠️ Match both maturity and currency.

**Line three: the index's annualized return — the stock-picking benchmark.**

Did picking stocks beat buying the index and ignoring it? The harshest line, and the most necessary.

⚠️ **This line is unusually high in US markets.** The Nasdaq's long-run annualized return far exceeds most markets, which means two things: your third line is higher than other people's, and **so is your opportunity cost** — underperforming the index costs more here than elsewhere.

## ⚠️ But line one has a large blind spot: CPI excludes house prices

**CPI does not track house purchase prices.** This isn't a flaw in any one country's statistics — it's the **international standard**. The CPI manuals from the UN, the World Bank and the ILO all classify housing as a **fixed asset** rather than a consumption good. Buying a house acquires an asset; it doesn't consume one. No country's CPI tracks house prices.

The US approach is **Owners' Equivalent Rent (OER)** — "what would this owner-occupied home rent for?"

📌 **One Taiwan-US difference worth noting**: OER carries substantial weight in the US CPI, so **US CPI is actually more sensitive to housing costs than Taiwan's** — Taiwan's rent index has been remarkably flat for years while house prices ran far ahead, leaving a wider gap.

In either market, **CPI measures the price of consumption spending, not the price of assets.** It isn't wrong; it's answering a different question.

**The sensible response is to add a line rather than discard CPI:**

- **CPI** → is my day-to-day consumption purchasing power being eroded?
- **A house price index** (the FHFA House Price Index or Case-Shiller in the US) → am I keeping up with asset prices?

⚠️ **Don't call a house price index "real inflation."** It measures asset prices, answering a different question from consumer prices. Merging the two repeats the "risk-free rate = inflation" mistake in a new place.

## Running through it once

1. Go to [My Performance](/twr?lang=en) and enter net deposits and month-end assets month by month for your **cumulative and annualized TWR**. Stored in your browser only.
2. The "Beat the Index" section on the same page shows the last two years of Nasdaq returns with **that year's CPI** beside them — lines one and three on the same screen.
3. Go to [Rates and Purchasing Power](/macro?lang=en) for the bond yield closest to your holding period, as line two.

Using ten-year annualized US inflation of 3.32% as the baseline:

- 3.3% annualized: real return around **0%** — merely preserving value
- 8% annualized: real return around **+4.5%**
- 12% annualized: real return around **+8.4%**

That tells you far more than "I made 15% this year."

## Three easy mistakes

**① Your inflation assumption changes a lot with the window.** Ten-year US annualized inflation is 3.32%, but that window contains the 2021–2023 surge. Different windows give visibly different numbers. **Always state the period.**

**② Cross-market comparisons carry a currency layer.** See the Taiwan-US section above.

**③ The "risk-free rate" is not the rate you can actually get.** A bond yield is a market price, not your broker's cash sweep rate nor a money market fund's return after fees. It works as a **benchmark**, not as **your available option**.

## In one sentence

**The risk-free rate works as a rough proxy for inflation, but don't treat them as the same thing.**

They are related because the nominal rate already contains expected inflation. They differ because the real interest rate sits between them — and that is the number you should care about.

Split them into three lines and you can answer three different questions about any investment: did it beat prices, did it justify giving up a risk-free return, and did it beat the index?
